## Elegant Linux

### Know about Linux

My acquaintance with Linux probably began with idle chats with classmates in high school. Back then I was firmly set on an academic research path and had little interest in computers beyond algorithms, so I never looked into it much. It wasn't until sophomore year that I developed a desire to understand Linux, because I was hugely interested in LLM/ML/DL at the time. That interest pushed me to learn how to use GPUs and servers for code simulation and model training, and once you get into that, it's hard not to mention Linux. I happen to be the kind of person who likes reinventing wheels and poking at how things work, so I naturally started with some basic shell commands and simple logic.

Speaking of which, it's worth a digression. At first I only liked learning algorithms—taking courses like CS50x/CS61ABC—and in sophomore year I set up my own blog. But I knew next to nothing about tools like WSL/Docker/VMs. It was only later that I realized using Linux could greatly optimize the performance of this machine and simplify my life to a large extent (at least I got control over almost the entire computer). So by the end of 2025 I had basically moved all my work onto NixOS (the Linux distro I currently use).

### Learn to Use Linux

My use of Linux matured gradually while I was dealing with distributed systems. Strictly speaking, it was only in the second half of this year that I became reasonably familiar with the command line and could basically solve all the problems I ran into on my own, instead of watching videos and the like.

From doing the distributed systems labs, it was pretty obvious that the people around me were terrible at hands-on computer work. Many of them couldn't use the command line at all, nor search tools to learn on their own and fix bugs; you could basically say they were about as clueless as it gets (a beginner is someone who knows nothing but knows where to find answers, not a giant baby who knows nothing at all). Only then did I feel that computer science education in China is a complete mess. The teachers I'd met before would actually incorporate popular American textbooks and adapt foreign courses, but the teacher I got in junior year was truly beyond words.

> For people who like tinkering, Linux is an inexhaustible treasure trove. You can lose yourself in beautifying your desktop, or of course try every way to dig into the kernel; you can create the tools you use from scratch through code.

As for installing and configuring Linux, you could say I caught a good era. I can easily install almost any well-known distro through a GUI, and there are almost no bugs to fix. If the input method blows up or the hardware drivers blow up, it's almost always because I'm not using the latest tutorials and docs. Even Tencent Meeting got a native Linux version this year (solving the screen-sharing problem that had plagued me for ages; before, I had to rely on another project, and that project got archived this year too).

You could say Linux is a ~~toy~~ operating system that can genuinely shock a person. I can almost make this assertion (though I personally don't like assertions): if someone has zero interest in how Linux works or in tinkering with it, their curiosity about CS is basically very low, and I can't imagine what it's like for them to work in a CS-related field (in fact, a lot of the CS students around me will probably graduate without knowing what GitHub is or what Linux is—like that "Who is Adam?" line from NIPS).

> [!info] Some thoughts on operating systems
> I don't know if anyone else feels this way, but reinstalling your OS is a great way to figure out what the center of your life is. Reinstalling means formatting your drive (even if only part of it); you really do personally kill every file on that drive with your own hands, turning them from living information into meaningless sequences of 0s and 1s. I've reinstalled my system about three or four times (not counting failed attempts), and it gives me a good grasp of my current workflow. I no longer agonize over whether to keep software I rarely use.

### Thinking About More Linux

Mainly, I should focus on the following areas:

1. Install Arch Linux once to get a taste of the traditional "I use Arch, btw"; I can use one of my existing drives, and ideally also try CachyOS for its silky-smooth gaming and to experience the AUR
2. Install several desktops and switch between them—probably Hyprland/Niri/KDE/GNOME
3. Try packaging something for Nix myself
4. Learn the Nix language more deeply, though I actually don't think there's any must-learn reason for this language unless you're doing deep applications

As for why I want to install Arch Linux once, the reason is simple: Arch Linux's wiki and packages are extremely well done, and its philosophy is quite radical. I'm curious too—though personally I don't like that unstable feeling, which is why the system I use now is NixOS (u1s1, if you accidentally blow up your computer, reinstalling it only costs you the time of running sudo nixos-rebuild switch and the time to open your proxy software or configure domestic mirrors).

As for multiple desktops, that's easy to understand too—I'm all about how things look, and visuals matter to me. Items 3 and 4 are things you have to do if you want to participate deeply in this community.

### The Great Blue Bird

This work is so great that I had to give it its own heading just to praise it!!!

「神様、どうして鳥籠の開け方を教えてくれたの？」

## Statistics/Math Theory Learning

What I mainly studied this year was high-dimensional statistics. I worked through a very famous French book, [HDS](http://high-dimensional-statistics.wikidot.com/). I don't know why it's so hard to download now; maybe there was a copyright issue and it got taken down after complaints.

I read quite a few papers, almost all dealing with high dimensions. The curse of dimensionality means any two variables you pick are almost independent, so statistical research runs into some difficulties (why am I even saying this, as if anyone's going to read it). Then there are the basic mathematical tools, like real analysis and functional analysis. I actually find these much simpler than the weird structure of those statistics courses, and I'm more comfortable learning them. Going forward I should keep strengthening my analysis foundations in a targeted way.

After that, some developments in DL. In the end I couldn't resist the temptation and stepped into interpretability myself. I was roughly following some results from the famous company Anthropic, then went through some courses and books on my own to get started, but I don't feel the results were very good.

A good reference is probably the blog of a big shot in this area: [Neelnanda](https://www.neelnanda.io/).

Although I made no real progress in my own research this year, I've got some ideas about what to study next. Once you actually read some papers, you find that the mathematical tools statistics uses aren't all that deep (at least in the areas I've explored). As for empirical processes, semiparametric efficiency, and those Inequality Engineers, I'm still skeptical—it feels like they're super-glued together from all sorts of places.

Next year I still want to try to read some more theoretical books as a supplement:

+ Van der Vaart
+ HDP
+ Statistical Inference
+ HDS

I don't know how to put it—what I learned this year was indeed more CS-ish. I even read through an Introduction to Algorithms and encountered a lot of algorithms; it felt quite interesting. I also genuinely discovered that I'm bad at memorizing these weird exercises and at remembering strange conclusions. I'd rather do things slowly; the eager-for-quick-results approach probably isn't for me. From now on I'll never again choose a course that's all memorization...
