# One Year of Linux

Without really noticing, I've now used Linux as my daily system for a year. I think it's been an important part of my college life: constantly tinkering with operating systems, looking for interesting software to put on my machine, finding bugs and filing issues with developers (in practice, a lot of projects require you to reproduce the problem on their latest version before they'll take a report). Along the way my understanding of computers kept getting deeper. The computer is basically in my hands every day, and I always spend some of that time trying to do things I couldn't do before. Of course, it has also made my social isolation worse (hahaha).

I'm about to be a senior and I have no classes left to take, so almost nothing else is weighing on me. This summer I happily read a lot of CS books, and I have what I consider a key ideas post in the works (stuck in labor), so I figured I'd write this memoir — or anniversary post — first and slack off a bit.

## Prologue: it started with a Minecraft server

My first contact with Linux was actually because of the idea of a server. That was around my first year of high school, playing Minecraft and Terraria with a bunch of friends. Inevitably we wanted to play together online, and a server was the key ingredient. At the time we mostly relied on classmates who already knew some tech, who set up a server with BT Panel for us to play on. I was very curious about how it worked, and that's how I first heard of Linux. But the Linux environment back then was nowhere near as good as it is now. And I was deep into math and physics at the time, interested in computers only insofar as writing simple algorithm programs for online judges (which is probably true of most people who start programming), so the system side of things felt interesting but not quite intoxicating enough, and I let it drop.

Then I got to college, and all kinds of brain-rotting media, more and more pointless socializing, and more and more formalism made me sick to my stomach. Bit by bit, the joy I could feel in the world got smaller, so out of spite I browsed more forums and communities looking for new stimulation — domestic ones first, then foreign ones — and gained more open thinking and a broader view of the world. Even then I was still fixated on math and wanted to transfer into the math department, because for me the most exciting thing was to produce some nontrivial result and publish it, and I wasn't keen on short-term gains. I wanted to do something long-term as my career, and math and the other natural sciences were the obvious choice. Or rather, math has always been what I love; even though it's probably impossible now for me to go into pure math or even mathematical statistics research, I don't regret the enormous energy and time cost I spent on mathematical and statistical theory.

> [!note] Solving problems on your own
> Learning to solve problems on your own is a very important ability, whether in math or in fixing system bugs, because there's a good chance you're the only person using Linux and nobody can help you. And most platforms will only offer terrible advice in response to your request for help; very few people can directly propose a plan for your need and solve the problem (this is a terrifying thing, and I've seen it in math students too — I cannot understand the behavior of not answering someone's question directly but instead first passing judgment, and outright denying someone's need. This is perhaps why Linux, and most open source projects and open source activity, isn't popular in China).

After much deliberation, I did transfer into statistics, which would let me get closer to computers or, to use the fashionable word, AI. AI is the biggest opportunity, one anybody with eyes can see, so I bet on the future and on my own interests (at the time I thought very simply: statistics is what combines math and computers, and what gives you a chance to take part in the AI wave — and thanks to how weak the engineering side of my school is, I had almost no hesitation between pure computer science and statistics).

Then came a year of study. I took part in an activity organized by my department, roughly gathering a bunch of people who wanted to produce some results; looking back it seems like a not very successful research experience project. I was never optimistic about it, and I thought that for undergraduates the experience matters more, so I never intended to publish a paper (though a lot happened later that made me resolve to quit, to stop wanting to go into statistics academia and instead throw myself more wholeheartedly into computer science). I spent about a year in one professor's group, and I can say I really did encounter a lot of new currents of thought, learned the outline of AI research, studied a large amount of different theory (both the novel but illusory kind and the traditional but still effective kind), read many papers and reproduced a fair amount of code. Objectively, my abilities did improve.

## Turning point: Ubuntu on a hard drive

I seem to have gone off topic, but it was in exactly this process (reproducing code) that I came into contact with Linux again — or more precisely: It's Fxxking Ubuntu 22.04. A huge number of AI projects need this version, and this version is absurdly old; some packages don't work well and compatibility problems come up. Because I wanted to experience it myself, I installed Ubuntu 24.04 on a hard drive (I think it's better than both 26 and 22, and it's worth mentioning here: thanks to my habit of buying SSDs to store systems and data/anime, I now have two SSDs, and selling one of them would recoup the principal for both — with even a little profit).

![Last summer's vacation](https://img.eurekaimer.icu/20261018100051-0016.webp)

Ubuntu's authority in deep learning is beyond doubt: it has the best Nvidia support, there are tons of solutions online, it's the most widespread distro and a good choice for beginners (this was the consensus in every post I saw at the time; later, of course, I found out that with AI's help, installing any distro isn't much of a problem). Then came fiddling with the desktop environment and related software (the input method has to be installed, the browser has to be configured, proxy tools are still indispensable). To sum it up in one sentence: I basically made Ubuntu on KDE look like an Apple product.

Another reason I dared to tinker like that: I had a desktop machine set up as well, so even if the install failed there was a fallback, which made me unusually bold — I just kept sending questions to AI, which at the time was the relatively advanced Gemini. But later I discovered that a lot of low-end training resources can be had for free, and that anything needing a bigger GPU should be rented on a server (in other words, installing Ubuntu on the local machine wasn't so necessary, and my GPU at the time, an entry-level RTX 5060, wasn't good enough to justify configuring a system specially for it).

Then came a very important turning point. Because I'd bought the laptop too cheaply, combined with Windows 11's habit of shitting all over everything, my laptop became extremely laggy, so I made up my mind to install Linux locally instead of going through a hard drive — because a hard drive takes up a slot, and then I wouldn't be able to plug in two drives to copy things, and carrying a drive around every day is a bit showy anyway. So I deployed it locally, which made choosing a distro an important decision.

## Decision: distro battle royale

> [!tip] No distro is above another
> A reminder: no distro is above another, there's only whether it matches you. The distro you're comfortable with is the best distro; instead of fussing over distro features, go accumulate actual skill.

For more of my criticism of operating systems, see the [essay](https://www.eurekaimer.icu/notes/WebSource/%E8%AE%A1%E7%AE%97%E6%9C%BA%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6(CS)%E8%B5%84%E6%BA%90%E6%B1%87%E6%80%BB/#_4) on my notes site.

I watched a great many videos to help decide, on Bilibili and on YouTube, and in the end I chose by elimination. First, it absolutely could not be Ubuntu, because I know full well the harm of snap and how boring that system is. Second, it could not be Arch or its derivatives (at least not Arch itself), because Arch users show up under every Linux topic every day posting "btw" and it's very annoying (even though I ended up using CachyOS and find it very comfortable). Finally, it should preferably be fairly stable, because I don't much like adapting to the newest version features — perhaps I've been hurt too many times by Python and AI research. So one distro became the answer to everything: NixOS.

> [!info] NixOS isn't that hard to get into
> Many people say NixOS has a very high barrier: you have to learn an extra language and you need a good network environment. But I'd say that, as someone who has installed NixOS about five or six times, the install only requires knowing how to add a mirror or share a network over a USB cable — installation is very easy. As for the rest, if you don't want to learn the nix language, just have a large model write it. Current large models have basically no trouble writing config files, and by reading the config they write you quickly become familiar with the corresponding interfaces and syntax (it's essentially a very simple language, not at all the steep learning curve most people describe; the steepness basically came from earlier models not being good enough and the documentation being too sparse). If you're not a purist, you don't even look at it: state the requirement, rebuild, and as long as it works, it works.

Through declarative configuration it guarantees that almost everything about the system can be configured with your own nix code, everything is within your control, and it has a good generations mechanism ensuring that once you have a stable build, you basically don't have to worry about the system breaking. And you can manage the config files with git, reusing them across all your computers. That really appealed to me, because I always have this upgrade anxiety: electronics can't last very long, they will be replaced eventually, and at that point iteration cost and controllability become very important (anyone who has changed phones knows how time-consuming it is to reinstall software and make sure all your files and data have been migrated and copied). So I went straight for NixOS, and a year later I'm still using it to write this very post.

I can say it really is stable — the distro I've used the longest. Apart from rebuilds when I did my own big updates, I never had to worry about the system breaking. But it has quite a few downsides in practice. One obvious one is that it doesn't follow the FHS layout — you should know many programs depend on FHS to run, and not following FHS means you have to put in more effort than on an ordinary distro when using software (different dynamic linker paths), and it also affects the normal operation of many toolchains, e.g. setting up environments takes extra fine-tuning and management cost (extremely obvious with Python, relatively less friction with Rust and Go). Then there's the space taken by keeping multiple versions around: though NixOS already optimizes this by pointing to the same version instead of storing multiple copies, it still takes up a fair amount, which hurts my file storage (512 GB really can't take it). And then there's the package problem: there appear to be many packages, but most are orphaned and of poor quality, and in practice they're not as good as the Flatpak and AppImage versions.

The good side is that in the process of digging into the system I gradually gained some control over it, and eventually kicked Windows out of my ecosystem and moved the desktop to CachyOS too. As for why CachyOS, the reason is simple: good performance optimizations, a dedicated kernel optimization that's convenient for gaming, and it's an Arch Linux derivative so the software is new enough (by this point my thinking had shifted enough: I'm someone who's on a computer every day, so why should I fear rolling breakage? Embracing change is the right attitude, and only by using the newest software can I give timely feedback to upstream maintainers and help their open source. As for the tired old stories of rolling breakage and AUR poisoning, I think that as long as you manage the software you choose sensibly, read PKGBUILDs carefully to avoid poisoned packages, and install a backup LTS kernel and snapshots, rolling breakage is fairly difficult these days). **As for stability, ordinary distros all have it; it's just a matter of granularity. That kind of NixOS-style stability is unnecessary for individuals and even for the vast majority of companies; even in cases with a hard requirement for atomic stability, you can absolutely combine it with the nix language instead of bringing in a whole distro.**

Given how capable agents are now, I don't think writing a quick install script to help restore an environment is difficult anymore (this somewhat encroaches on NixOS's biggest selling point, and it's also why I've resolved to drop NixOS in the future). Still, I have to say: precisely because I used NixOS for a long time, I'm quite sensitive about control over the system. Whatever software gets installed, I make the agent record it in the corresponding documentation and add a database to maintain it, which has effectively kept the growth of information entropy in check.

> [!warning] Community ecosystem
> Let me add one point: another very important reason for choosing the Arch family is the community ecosystem — yes, the community ecosystem. Even though there are plenty of missionary brats, there really are technical experts who earnestly help others, and the problems you run into can be solved quickly (for example, whenever Arch comes up people say how great ArchWiki is, but I'd say with a large model the workload is cut down considerably; having AI retrieve and feed back is more efficient than chewing through the Wiki yourself — the Wiki has, so to speak, already been parameterized). NixOS, for certain reasons (there seems to have been some community politics), isn't really a united community, and it has a large number of low-quality packages and issues nobody looks at. Take my own case: I hit a bug using some software's nix package and reported it to the corresponding project's repo; the maintainer replied that they didn't have the relevant environment and didn't know what the problem was. Later I found out the packager's skills really weren't up to par: they introduced some extra errors while packaging, and some problems long fixed in the latest version weren't followed up by the nix packager either.

## Field notes: desktops, editors, and virtual machines

When it comes to using Linux, the first thing most people notice is the **desktop environment**. Beautifying your desktop and posting a fastfetch screenshot has become the driving force for many people to use Linux. That's not a bad thing — on the contrary, it has greatly promoted Linux adoption and even the development of desktop environments. I even think that when I got into Linux last year I happened to step right onto a turning point of the era: LLMs were there to help, desktop environments had been greatly optimized, good tutorials were springing up like mushrooms, and even compatibility layers had improved a lot (see: [How to play Arknights on NixOS](https://www.eurekaimer.icu/notes/Tech/Wheels/%E5%A6%82%E4%BD%95%E4%BD%BF%E7%94%A8NixOS%E7%8E%A9%E6%98%8E%E6%97%A5%E6%96%B9%E8%88%9F/)). Catching a good era really is a lucky thing.

I put some effort into fiddling with desktop environments too. As I mentioned, at first it was the Mac-flavored KDE, and then because I needed a WM with vim-mode support — that is, a tiling desktop manager with free splitting and keyboard-driven flow — I went straight for the then very popular Niri paired with Noctalia, which has dedicated adaptations, forming the 3N setup (NixOS + Niri + Noctalia). Once you have a Linux environment, the other thing you have to try is vim/neovim-style text editors; cutting down mouse use greatly speeds up development, and after using it for a long time I've configured my own keybinding system as well.

Then there's how to manage your projects with Git and adapt them to the FHS layout, so you can trim local storage nicely and save a lot of time on future migrations. And then my frantic preference for CLI and TUI: because GUI software is so inconvenient on NixOS, when I develop things myself I lean toward adding CLI/TUI, and toward statically compiled languages like Go/Rust to reduce system friction.

And then getting to know KVM/QEMU. I'd always known about the QEMU project but never used it (at one point I helped a classmate set up the environment for an embodied intelligence project, which used the ffmpeg library; some upstream changes meant the library wasn't compatible at the time, so we had to work around it. Only later did I learn how formidable that library's author is — his other work, QEMU, is likewise a world-renowned open source project). After learning a bit more, out of curiosity about virtual machine performance under Linux, I installed KVM/QEMU/virtual machine manager and successfully filled in the last piece of the all-Linux puzzle: light Office use and things like screen sharing in Tencent Meeting.

(For various reasons the Office-related packages under Linux really aren't good — all kinds of font drift and display problems. Tencent Meeting is even worse: at best you can share your screen once from the local machine, and the moment the network wobbles and the share drops, sharing again is guaranteed to fail; watching someone else share is fine though. Compare that with Feishu from the ByteDance family — since it's an alumni company I've gotten three years of free use out of their apps, and it feels much more conscientious: screen sharing is no trouble at all, and even the smoothness feels better than Tencent Meeting. Universe factory > goose factory, does anyone get it? Turns out tech really is for the young.)

## Epilogue: just a tool, all roads lead to the same place

Many people keep Linux at arm's length, thinking it's too hard to use and only highly skilled programmers can manage it. In reality that's completely wrong. In the world of computers everything is engineering, and engineering can't come into being apart from people; even LLMs work under human commands. So by the theory of the return of intelligence, there certainly can't be a world-renowned project so brilliant that an ordinary person can't use it after putting in the effort. Facts support this too: many people just keep installing different distros and working out how to make the system pretty, never really getting into the underlying logic or using Linux's advantages to do development work — yet they're still very happy, because they treat installing and configuring Linux as a change of pace in life, a rare bit of fun. People don't find things difficult at the time if they find them fun, so they quickly got a return too: they really did end up using Linux.

Thinking one layer deeper, the open source community is probably the thing in this world closest to the form of communism in my heart: the means of production (source code) are public, anyone can take, modify, and redistribute them, and the fruits of labor (software) are distributed according to need, so almost anyone can afford to use them. It's completely different from the communism I was fed growing up, the kind that exists only in textbooks — this one is real, built brick by brick at night by countless people at their keyboards. When you fork a project, submit a PR, or just leave one useful reply under someone else's issue, you become a member of this community. In the commercial closed-source world, companies lock code in a safe; in the open source world, everyone lays the results out to share, and whoever needs them takes them. This spirit makes me think idealism probably isn't dead.

> [!quote] On engineering classmates
> For most engineering classmates outside computer science, though, since most software is tightly bound to Windows, there's basically no way for them to fully commit; virtual machines are still a bit of a hassle after all.

Writing to this point, my enthusiasm for writing has actually faded, because there's really nothing left to write. For computers, the operating system is undoubtedly important, but ultimately it should serve the user — it's just a tool. Different distros all end up in much the same place in the end (just some small feature differences); what matters is how you use it to develop and produce. In that respect, I'm both a little afraid and very glad to know: I still have a long road ahead.
