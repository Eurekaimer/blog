# 2025 Winter Break Review

## Preface

I finally got a break, so here's my overdue winter break recap. I like to start my recaps with a rough qualitative judgment, then look at what I did in quantitative terms and revise that first judgment. This one is no different.

Broadly speaking, I did the following:

- [x] Watched the first few CS50x lectures and did some problem sets
- [x] Spent two weeks learning the basics of mathematical modeling and gave the MCM/ICM everything I had
- [x] Tweaked my personal site to give it a few useful features and resources
- [x] Came across some good online math courses and textbooks and decided to keep going after classes resumed
- [x] Improved my note-taking system

## The start of the break

### What I did

The first phase was the week or so after I got home. I was full of ambition, but I had a decent idea of how much self-control and how little efficiency I'd have on vacation. I kept the workload light: watched a few CS50x lectures, finished the first two problem sets, sorted my GitHub repositories a bit, and moved some files around.

I'd made plans to do CS61A with a younger student over the break, but his vacation started later. I figured I'd finish CS50x myself first so I could keep up with the online course. That turned out to be one of the more interesting and worthwhile parts of the break. It was my first time doing an open course from abroad in computer science. I'd watched more math courses, but they hadn't felt the same, with a course site and supporting resources the way CS50x has. The course really is good. The instructor speaks English fluently (kidding) and teaches with a lot of enthusiasm. The Codespace loaded a bit slowly (I later found out my room was far enough away to have bad Wi-Fi), but I still loved watching.

### A little rant

Taking an open course from abroad is a good way to get a sense of how the world is changing culturally. There are faces from all over in the classroom, most of the volunteers the instructor calls up are of Asian descent, and Chinese faces are everywhere! It's also demystifying. These courses aren't magic and Harvard students aren't one hundred percent geniuses. The pace isn't particularly fast, but the problem sets do take a fairly deep understanding of the material. I saw lots of things like the exercises on Chinese online judges. Who invented that exercise where you print a diamond, anyway? I might look it up someday. I've been writing it over and over since the first year of high school, all the way through sophomore year of college. Nested loops all the way down.

As an open course, it probably puts more effort into show-and-tell, like the AI tools and physical demonstrations that courses abroad really seem to love. I'm more partial to straight theory. The simple algorithms aren't that hard to understand or in need of that many words. I'd have gotten much more out of it if I'd actually been starting from zero.

My last gripe is about the course site itself. Having made one, I don't think it's that hard to put up a rough website for slides and notes. Yet here we still have to use messaging apps like WeChat for that. Feishu has something like a document library that might make it easier. It's worth asking ourselves why, even without so many hands-on demos, our theory teaching is so dull and covers less than a general-education course. That's on us to think about.

Joke: CS50x does have moments where you can slip out to pee. It's just that long (I guess)...

## The middle of the break

### A lovely sea of mathematics

After writing some code, I still found I preferred the beauty of working through pure mathematical theory. I don't dislike coding. It has an engineering appeal of its own, but math is more my thing. I got a fair bit out of reading some real analysis. Folland has a lot of depth, and 齐震宇 at Taipei University is a gem. His *Analysis* is especially good and clear. He didn't account for how bad I am, though, and seems to assume most people have a better grounding in analysis than they do. The introduction to complex variables may jump ahead a little too fast. But it isn't a complex analysis course, and he did say it was just a brief introduction, so on the whole that's a small fault in a very good course.

I improved my note system and got a lot more comfortable taking notes in Markdown.

I started looking for a competition to try and found teammates for the MCM/ICM on the campus marketplace. That was followed by two weeks of self-study. I mostly picked up Python again, downloaded a lot of software, and read a good number of Outstanding Winner papers. When we split up the roles, I chose to do the modeling. I hadn't expected to get the chance to compete, and I was scared of letting the others down, so all I could do was work like mad to catch up. I watched two core online courses and used the SPSSPRO site's documentation as a reference to learn a lot of the relevant algorithms.

Note: I did make one mistake here. We should've agreed up front on the kind of problem we'd pick and worked toward it together. There plainly wasn't time to learn everything. I think I got through over thirty algorithms and models in two days. It was painful, but I was happy, and I was making progress every hour.

The contest itself deserves a short section of its own.

### ICM/MCM

One funny thing is that I'm a mathematical statistics student, but all the useful statistics courses were still a semester away. I basically charged in with a very cursory grounding in mathematical analysis, higher algebra, and probability. Perhaps I got a little taste of statistics anyway. It felt pretty good, haha.

Then came the contest. The three of us looked up sources, read code, debugged, and thought about models until we lost all sense of time. After three days we'd written a 25-page paper. I learned a lot. It might be one of the more meaningful things I've done at university (though nothing could ever be as unforgettable as the exam to switch majors). I learned about plenty of packages and models. After the contest I left some math group chats. (Were those even math chats? The atmosphere was pretty foul.) I really can't stand what some people say online. For discussing a problem, go to a community that deals with it. (I highly recommend MSE.)

## The end of the break

I went back to campus fairly early and worked on my site, which is where you're reading this. The plugins broke, so I got fed up and rebuilt the whole thing. I set up MkDocs in a Python virtual environment, added a bunch of plugins, and changed the front end. At least the site looked a little more respectable. I'll probably use HTML to make the home page better later. There's still too much text and it isn't particularly comfortable to look at.

## One last thing

I talked with a few senior students. I think I have some strengths, but I'm lacking in plenty of ways too. I ought to take more specialized statistics courses and get a better grounding in the theory. Then I fretted a bit over what to do next. I hadn't expected my GPA to be good enough that I could consider applying to another university through recommended grad admission.

I hope real analysis and mathematical statistics are kind to me this semester.

Q.E.D.
