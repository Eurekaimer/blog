# Workflow v5.2 (last updated 2026-09-13)

## Theory

The "theory" discussed here is a fairly abstract layer: why to study this way, in what state to study, and why to use tools this way. Once this layer is clear, the specific tools and operations below have a basis.

### Introduction

> [!quote] 沃兹基硕德
> When a person goes through a long stretch of busyness, their brief leisure is often not spent resting, but reflecting on how to revise their workflow so that the next stretch of busyness can end in less time.

If you're a person with logical thinking, or someone with a strong case of neat-freak syndrome, you'll very much want to systematize and modularize everything about yourself — that's what makes it easy to maintain and extend (to put it in engineering terms). The same goes for study methods and workflows. Below I'll first explain the conceptual level, then give the workflow I'm currently using.

Generally speaking, the way we study from the day we start school until we graduate high school can be summed up as one simple theoretical system: GTD (Getting Things Done). It is an efficient time-management and workflow method, proposed by David Allen and widely used by individuals and organizations. GTD's core idea is that by recording and managing all tasks, you free tasks from your brain, reducing anxiety and improving efficiency. Its basic steps are as follows:

> This is only a very simple explanation, not a detailed introduction, because this method doesn't work for me and I don't much like it. I mention it only because schools mainly cultivate this theoretical system.

1. **Capture**: Record all information and tasks that need handling in a "raw pile" or "inbox" — whether emails, ideas, or to-dos, collect them all without omission.
2. **Clarify**: Sort and organize the collected information, ordering it by the nature of the task, deadline, and priority, so that every task gets appropriate handling.
3. **Organize**: Break tasks into actionable steps and schedule them at suitable times. This may include setting reminders, creating subtasks, or planning specific time blocks to complete tasks.
4. **Reflect**: Check and review the task list regularly to make sure nothing is missed, and adjust priorities and plans as needed.
5. **Engage**: Focus on the task at hand, doing one thing at a time until it's done. During execution you can use tools like the Pomodoro timer to improve focus and efficiency.

With the GTD method, a person can manage their time effectively and reduce stress at work and in life, thereby improving productivity and quality of life. GTD emphasizes the balance between "recording" and "executing", making sure all tasks are properly managed without overburdening the brain.

The process itself isn't complicated; the difficulty lies in sustaining it over the long term, not in understanding it.

This is actually similar to differentiating a Task to find a measure, sorting it into categories, and then taking a Lebesgue integral over it (doge). The key information point is that you need a sensible **classification system and organizational scheme**, plus repeated review and execution: a high volume of memorization and training. This method is quite traditional; most high school students and people who have never polished a workflow basically use this simple method too. In practice it's just the process of making a plan, previewing and reviewing, and then staying focused.

But I've found that this slightly mechanical way doesn't suit me; for me it has some big problems.

+ When there are too many tasks, even with external records I keep thinking about them, so it fails to reduce anxiety
+ This way of working gives me poor control over my workload, so I can't control my efficiency either
+ Focused execution can also miss fleeting opportunities, such as certain courses, talks, and events
+ Too many variables; **adjusting priorities is extremely complicated**
+ People with wide interests keep adding new todolists until the system collapses
+ It doesn't give a complete way to handle to-dos — that is, **it isn't concrete enough**

> An addition for the new year: as someone who loves learning and may have ADHD, I can't quite keep myself to a strictly linear plan, so rotating between different tasks while counting the total may be a more suitable method.

These problems show that the layer of "managing tasks" doesn't produce results by itself. What actually works is something lower down: how to learn, in what state to learn, and how to practice.

### Self-study

**Self-study should be the most efficient**, and within self-study, reading is more efficient than online courses. When the content is the same, prefer reading; but if it's a 61A situation where both reading and lectures exist, you can watch the course first for a rough idea and then read the book. On one hand, reading consumes a lot of mental energy and high-intensity study is hard to sustain; on the other hand, watching video lectures is fairly relaxing, which helps maintain your state and then extend your study time.

> I've tested this and consider it exactly right

Don't hesitate: if a teacher teaches badly, you must self-study. Most teachers teach worse than self-studying!

> A year later: every teacher teaches worse than self-study. The only use of a teacher is, after your first pass, to see whether they can offer some higher-level guidance in thinking.

Once the study method is settled, whether you can stick with it depends largely on your state.

### Handling mindset

First, deal with anxiety. If you reduce life to an operations-research optimization problem with limited resources, then the basic idea of this workflow is how to obtain the plan with the highest quantified score in the shortest time and finish it. So you need priority classification and some details given directly: **the less you think about small chores, the more time you have to work**. This needs a caveat: after many things recently, life for me is no longer just an optimization problem; enjoying life matters more.

> Already enjoying my Gap Year, achieving WLB (Work Life Balance)

First you need to recognize one thing: in the environment you're in, people's ability should follow a roughly normal distribution with no outliers — no so-called geniuses. Such outliers shouldn't be in the same environment as you (basic statistical principle). Staying away from grand narratives is also a good way out of anxiety. Many people hold forth using so-called sociology and psychology; in fact, from my observation, in any environment, any system with more than 30 people (you can take 30 as a biostatistics reference) will definitely have more than 80% idiots, 10% ordinary people, and the slightly smarter ordinary people (No Genius!).

Next is the problem of **overcoming OCD and perfectionism**. My MBTI result is INTJ. I don't really believe in this stuff, but I do have a strong perfectionist streak. Through a lot of experimentation I've found that while this mindset helps overcome procrastination, it also increases internal friction. You can improve it with multiple goals — a baseline goal, a higher goal, and a perfectionist goal — so that once you hit the baseline you can relax a bit. That's also why this article isn't organized perfectly; it's made up of quite a lot of empiricism and small systems.

Finally, hobbies: keep the number of hobbies **under three** if possible, otherwise it deals a big blow to the whole system — you can't finish the basic workload, and you can't enjoy the hobbies well either. **Homework is best finished in class, and projects are best given a complete engineering workflow.**

With your state sorted out, only then comes putting your hands to work.

### Practice training

This should be the most important part of the entire workflow: **systematically training your practical ability**. The progress made through practice is also the fastest and greatest, no contest. For example, **for math courses, doing problems is the best means of mastering and consolidating**.

+ Finishing problem sets
	+ Don't look at the answers
	+ Pick out important problems, think them over repeatedly, and organize them with `LaTeX`
	+ Work them out and write on paper
+ Reading books
	+ Finish reading first and write notes only after you have the whole overview, otherwise it easily turns into copying the book
	+ Read at least two chapters first, otherwise you'll easily abandon it
+ Writing projects
	+ Do a project; it can be a reproduction or something original
	+ You must increase how much code you write

Note that all files used and produced should be backed up weekly to hard drives, cloud storage, and basic local storage — that is, **backup awareness**

> [!tip] The secret to higher completion rates
> Focus on doing one thing well. For example, after finishing homework, go ALL IN on one course first and learn that course through and through. Then, once you have a solid foundation, move on to the next course. This helps build confidence and keeps your notes from being so redundant.

> [!info] Dividing time
> Divide the day into three parts — morning, afternoon, and evening. Try to keep a normal sleep schedule; don't stay up late and don't work without sleeping or resting.

Everything above is training at the level of doing things, but the tools in your hands while doing them also shape your habits in turn, which leads to the next section.

### Keyboard-centric workflow

An extra section to record this year of keyboard-centric life. First, I've been using `Linux` as my daily system for a year now; for the related article, see [One year with Linux](https://www.eurekaimer.icu/blog/posts/summary/linux-%E4%BD%BF%E7%94%A8%E4%B8%80%E5%91%A8%E5%B9%B4%E7%BA%AA%E5%BF%B5/). Also, thanks to `Linux`'s nature, I've gradually gotten comfortable with the Shell. Second, I installed some keyboard-centric open-source software, most importantly `Sioyek`, a reader basically modeled on `vim mode`, which greatly inspired my ideas about software design; in many of my design projects this year I tried to use keyboard-driven patterns, keeping the mouse out of the process as much as possible. My full embrace of the keyboard came mainly in the summer of this year (2026), hunting down and installing lots of excellent `Neovim` plugins and practicing touch typing, which brought my typing speed to a usable level. Thanks to `Neovim`'s rich ecosystem, my current production and work environment has been configured to be extremely comfortable — I don't even want to open `VSCode` anymore!

+ **`Linux` as the foundation of the workflow**: A simple kernel and filesystem make the whole system readable, modifiable, and scriptable; a good shell puts that capability directly on the keyboard; and a highly customizable desktop means splitting screens, switching windows, and launching apps no longer need the mouse. What I use here is `Niri`, a tiling window manager with vim-mode support. Stack the three together and the efficiency gain is real — this is also why I ultimately kicked `Windows` out of my usage ecosystem and moved my desktop to `CachyOS` as well.
+ **The design philosophy of keyboard-driven work**: If you know a thing or two about `Vim`, or about other similar editors like `Emacs`, you'll know these tools were built from the start around the idea of letting users rely entirely on the keyboard, thereby reducing the mouse's damage to inspiration. Cutting down mouse use greatly speeds up development, and after long use I've also configured a keybinding system of my own.
+ **A path for practicing touch typing**: first get the key mapping down on `keybr`, then practice speed and accuracy on `monkeytype`, and use `10fastfingers` for timed comparisons. The "practice touch typing" mentioned above followed exactly this path; only once your speed is sufficient does the keyboard-centric workflow really run.
+ **The lightness and speed of `Neovim`**: My config is based on `Neovim` 0.12's native capabilities plus `lazy.nvim`; the theme is Kanagawa Wave, and `<leader>` is the space key. Common entry points include `<leader><space>` smart find, `<leader>e` file tree, `<leader>ff` find files, `<leader>fg` full-text search, `<leader>mr` toggle `Markdown` rendering, and `<leader>mi` toggle image display. The whole editing process happens in the terminal — no need to leave it. If you want to turn `Neovim` into a complete IDE, you can refer to the tutorial [Learn How To Use NeoVim As an IDE](https://programmingpercy.tech/blog/learn-how-to-use-neovim-as-ide/).
+ **Replacing `Obsidian`'s use cases**: The current division of labor is that all note-writing (Lecture Notes, reading notes) happens in `Neovim` in `Markdown`, while `Obsidian` has degraded into a preview tool for finished notes and a file management tool. The reason I chose `Markdown` is precisely its lightweight format that lets you jot down ideas quickly.

## Practice

After nearly six semesters of college life, I've decided, after much painful reflection, to merge all my current tools and skills into one system — workflow 5.2 below. I've tried to solve the problems of the previous workflows, and I hope it helps readers too.

### Problem summary

> Seize the principal contradiction of a thing and use it as the lever to solve the problem, and you can achieve twice the result with half the effort.

To iterate toward a stronger, more effective workflow, you first need to be clear about why the previous workflow was unsatisfying: identify the problems, think up corresponding countermeasures, and then update step by step. Below are some problems I think the old workflow had.

+ Too many tools, not unified enough; there's no way to integrate all materials nicely in one terminal (All in One). But piling all content into one piece of software is almost impossible and infeasible from a software-engineering standpoint, leading to extreme bloat and inefficiency.
+ **Time spent reinventing wheels exceeded time spent using them.** Concretely: I didn't use `Obsidian` that much, yet spent a lot of time beautifying its front end; also, keeping `LaTeX` and `Markdown` text in sync cost a lot of time. After migrating to `Neovim` this problem eased a lot.
+ Paper notes and electronic notes couldn't **complement each other**. The solution I ended up with: use paper notes to aid understanding, but always produce an electronic version for archiving and filing.
+ **Too little action**, too few results, while too much time went into thinking about the workflow. This is a common failing — over-indulging in methodology and forgetting to solve the original problem.
+ Worrying about gains and losses, indecision — shown in using and choosing a toolchain. The solution I ended up with: set aside some time for A/B tests, reach a conclusion, and choose a suitable workflow driven by needs.
+ ...

### File naming principles

> [!abstract] Naming system
> For ease of management, I've summarized a set of naming principles covering everything (math–statistics–computer science). The overall structure follows three levels — "course/project → chapter or session → specific file" — and each piece of content keeps only one classification system, avoiding building the same library twice.

+ Lecture Notes
	+ Course homepage: `课程名称(Lecture).md`, as the entry point and index for the course (e.g. `数理统计(Lecture).md`, `随机过程(Lecture).md`, `实变函数(Lecture).md`)
	+ Chapter pages: uniformly placed in the course directory's `Assets/`; choose one of two styles according to the teacher's style, and stay consistent within the same course
		+ Taught by chapter: `第N章 主题.md` or `Chapter N 主题.md`
		+ Taught by session: `LectureN 主题.md` or `LecN 主题.md`
	+ Office hours (problem sessions): marked with `OH`, in the same directory as the regular lecture notes
	+ Homework: generally no prefix needed; just use `HW1`. It can be pushed directly to a personal blog or WeChat public account.
		+ `LaTeX`: you can first copy the problems in `Obsidian` with the LaTeX Suite plugin and then quickly edit them into shape with a template; leave recognition to a multimodal model
		+ `JPG(PNG)`: for image-type (paper homework), consider extracting some valuable problems and converting them to `LaTeX` for storage
		+ `PDF`: OCR it, or extract the valuable parts and convert them for storage in `Obsidian`
+ Reading Notes
	+ Use "book title or author" as the base, supplemented by a special identifier code, joined with `-`, e.g. `茆-第五章 统计量及其分布`
	+ Use the chapter name as the note title for that chapter
	+ If it's a fairly convincing and classic textbook, you can use the author's name, e.g. `Folland`, `Stein`, `Durrett`
+ MOOCs
	+ Course homepage: `课程代码.md` (e.g. `CS61A.md`, `CS231n.md`)
	+ Session pages: `课程代码-Week-N.md` or `课程代码-LectureN 主题.md`, also placed under `Assets/`
+ Other common types for computer science courses
	+ `Lab`
	+ `Problem Set`
	+ `Project N-名称`
	+ `Week N`
+ Tags
	+ Write a type tag on the first line of each note for easy search and aggregation, e.g. `#工作流`, `#总结`, `#Linux`, `#工具`

The naming rules still need refinement going forward.

### Task ordering

The naming principles settle where things go; what you actually have to decide every day is which thing to do first.

The planning part: think one day ahead about the next day's main goals, then sort them by priority. Note the rule: `don't add tasks at will`. Tasks you want to add extra can only be added starting the next day at the earliest, and higher-priority tasks must be completed first.

Daily routine-type tasks can be given the highest priority; homework due that day is second-highest; coursework in your own major comes next; non-major study outside your personal training plan comes after that (CS, Japanese, complex analysis).

> [!tip] Example priority system
> 1. Daily English study
> 2. Exercise
> 3. Finish homework
> 4. Theoretical major coursework and coding practice
> 5. Study outside the major
> 6. Slay the Spire
> 7. Chatting and short videos

If self-control is insufficient, you need to add a penalty function (I'm a tryhard so I don't need one, doge)

Recently I introduced a new supervision mechanism, Todo mate (Google Play); learning with companions is also a form of supervision

> Postscript: for a cause you love and are passionate about, no supervision is needed — you'll work and study very hard on your own!

> [!tip] Concentrate force
> Based on a semester of practice, don't study too many subjects at once; at most handle three at a time, like real analysis, operations research and optimization, and mathematical statistics. And things like MOOCs shouldn't be placed above major courses, because your own professional strength is obviously the most important.

### Taking notes

Once tasks are ordered, what settles out during study has to land in notes.

I've thought about notes for a very long time. First, it's clear that study is basically inseparable from notes, because you'll forget sooner or later. Notes help you get back quickly to a time when you had a good grasp of something — like a growth accelerator — and they can also store details of techniques and algorithms for later lookup.

Then there are the notes written during daily study. For example, **mathematical statistics, operations research and optimization, and real analysis** — these obviously important core major courses — the teacher mostly follows only the textbook and the slides. Earlier, to ensure good continuity, I generally chose a pad rather than a computer as the recording tool for such notes; by now the tablet has fully degraded into a reader and all notes are recorded on the computer, which makes it even more necessary to design your own macros and plugins in advance so that the speed can keep up.

If for some course you read some extra books on your own and have some new ideas, then unless the difference in structure and system is very large, or the book is too famous and effective, I'd suggest editing them directly into the original Lecture Notes

> There are some problems here: for the sake of standardized classification, I always create a separate folder for notes on a given book, but the problem is that that field/that course should be a unified whole. Different books, even with different coverage and styles, always cover roughly the same content; this practice leads to a lot of duplicated content and wasted time.

The solution I ended up with is to take one book as the standard, and then patch and complete the knowledge gained from other books on top of that standard.

For ease of publication, daily homework can mostly be written in `LaTeX`, for example mathematical analysis, advanced algebra, and real analysis. For mathematical statistics and operations research and optimization, since the problem quality isn't high, the volume of problems is fairly large, or the form of the solutions is hard to present (yes, simplex tableaux, I mean you), I study first and then record in `Obsidian`.

> I suggest recording in `Markdown` as much as possible and then using an LLM to convert it, because the `Markdown` format is a lightweight format that records thoughts very well and quickly, and it's also easy to write

> [!abstract] Recording principles
> 1. "Don't copy the book" is a false premise, because as a beginner it's barely possible to explain the entire core of a theorem or method in your own way, but books, thanks to repeated reading and multiple rounds of teaching, have fairly reliable correctness and depth. So copying the book can indeed increase understanding; just remember to update your notation later. Reaching a level where you understand and can apply it is what matters most.
> 2. `LaTeX` notes can be uploaded in sections; don't plan to upload everything at once after finishing, as that easily leads to procrastination. Upload to your site, public account, or other platforms as soon as a chapter is done.
> 3. Don't mess around with open problems.
> 4. Don't read very old references at random.

### Review and reflection

Notes are a record of the process; regular review and summary are another matter.

Currently I mainly write weekly reports and blog posts.

The weekly report includes:

1. Tasks completed this week
2. Tasks to complete next week
3. Reflection on the gap from the original plan
4. Reaching some conclusions, finding optimization points, then recording them; once enough has accumulated, **go back and think about whether they make sense**
5. Iterating the workflow again (only things that self-update won't fall behind)

Be sure to re-read your own weekly reports, spot common problems, and fix them.
